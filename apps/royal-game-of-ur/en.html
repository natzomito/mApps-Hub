Game rules

Goal: be the first to move all 7 of your pieces across the board and off the end of the track.

Board: 20 squares. Each player has 4 private starting squares and 2 private finishing squares, off-limits to the opponent. The middle row is 8 shared squares that both players cross — and where they can capture each other.

Dice: 4 tetrahedral binary dice (0 or 1 each), 0–4 pips total. A roll of 2 is most common; 0 and 4 are rarest.

Move: move one piece exactly the number of squares rolled — a piece from hand enters directly onto the square equal to the roll (within your 4 private starting squares).

Blocking: you cannot place a piece on a square occupied by your own piece.

Capture: on the shared middle-row squares, landing exactly on an opponent's piece sends it back to hand. Exception: the central rosette square is safe — no one can be captured or blocked there.

Rosettes: the 5 decorated squares (2 private per player + 1 shared) grant an extra roll to whoever lands on them.

Leaving the board: requires a roll that moves a piece exactly "off the board" — overshooting is not allowed, you must hit it exactly.

Miss: rolling 0 or having no legal move passes the turn with no action.

The 7-piece variant follows the reconstruction by British Assyriologist Irving Finkel (British Museum), based on a Babylonian cuneiform tablet from c. 177 BCE. Some simplified commercial sets use 5 pieces instead.

History of the game

In 1922, British archaeologist Leonard Woolley came across this game during excavations of the royal tombs of the ancient city of Ur, in present-day southern Iraq. The most famous surviving board, inlaid with mother-of-pearl, lapis lazuli and red limestone, is about 4,600 years old.

Judging by the finds, the game spread across the ancient Near East — boards have been discovered in present-day Iraq, Iran, Syria, Lebanon and Egypt, and even in Cyprus and Crete. Rulers and commoners alike played it; four very similar boards were found in Tutankhamun's tomb.

For thousands of years nobody knew exactly how it was played — the rules vanished along with the civilizations that created them. They were deciphered only by British Assyriologist Irving Finkel of the British Museum, who read a Babylonian cuneiform tablet written around 177 BCE by a scribe named Itti-Marduk-balatu. It described each square of the board as a kind of fortune, from which the logic of the track and the moves could be reconstructed.

The game fell out of use in late antiquity for unclear reasons, probably giving way to backgammon. Today it is largely unknown to the wider public — although in recent years enthusiasts, including in Iraq itself, have begun to revive it.
